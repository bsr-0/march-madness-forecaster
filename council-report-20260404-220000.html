Chairman's Verdict

Where the Council Agrees

Look-ahead bias is the primary validity threat. If Torvik files contain post-tournament ratings, every result is contaminated. The optimizer would be selecting brackets using information that wasn't available at selection time. This must be resolved before interpreting any result.

Multiple comparisons is real. p=0.027 across 6 tested modes does not clear the Bonferroni-corrected threshold of 0.0083. The result is suggestive but not confirmed.

17 years is a thin inference base with enormous variance. Tail metrics like P(top5%) are even noisier than mean rank at this sample size.

Where the Council Clashes

Information asymmetry: feature or flaw? The Contrarian and Outsider argue that opt_torvik's advantage comes from an unfair information edge that won't replicate. First Principles and the Executor counter that exploiting better information over the pool is the strategy — as long as the data was genuinely available pre-tournament.

Scale up or fix foundation first? The Expansionist wants to ensemble 3–4 Massey systems (from the 182 available) to kill catastrophic years. The Executor says validate the single-source result first — ensembling on a contaminated foundation compounds the error.

Is BestRank (159 vs 32.5) a problem? Depends on payout structure. If the pool pays top-5%, consistency wins. If it's winner-take-all, you need the outlier upside that a BestRank of 159 cannot provide.

Blind Spots

  • 17 annual observations is the entire inference base. No statistical technique can manufacture degrees of freedom that don't exist.
  • Opponent pool skill distribution is unexamined. The simulation assumes a fixed opponent model. Real pool skill levels are unknown and likely changing over time.
  • Catastrophic years have no mechanistic explanation. The worst years haven't been traced to specific bracket decisions or tournament dynamics.

Recommended Sequence (Gated)

  3. Diagnose catastrophic years before ensembling. Trace worst-performing years to specific bracket decisions.
  4. Resolve payout structure question. This determines whether MeanRnk, P(top5%), or P(1st) is the objective.

Advisor Responses

The Contrarian Contrarian

p=0.027 fails Bonferroni. The corrected threshold is 0.0083 for 6 comparisons, and this result doesn't clear it. You're looking at the best of 6 and calling it a finding.

Look-ahead bias is the killer. If the Torvik files contain end-of-season ratings — ratings that incorporate tournament performance — the model had future information baked into its predictions. Every metric in that table becomes meaningless.

The opponent model mismatch is damning. opt_torvik uses information that ESPN pool participants didn't have access to (or didn't use effectively). That's either an exploitable edge or an artifact of asymmetric backtesting — and you can't tell which until you audit the data.

Recommendation: Audit the data files. If they're post-tournament, stop. If pre-tournament, rerun with Bonferroni and see what survives.

The First Principles Thinker First Principles

The information asymmetry isn't a flaw — it's the entire point. A bracket pool is a competition. If your model uses information that opponents don't use (or use poorly), that's the definition of an edge. The question is whether that edge existed at prediction time.

The BestRank collapse from 32.5 (seed) to 159 (opt_torvik) suggests the optimizer is targeting the middle of the distribution — consistently good but never great. Whether this matters depends entirely on the payout structure.

Key question: What's the actual payout structure of the target pool? This determines whether MeanRnk, P(top5%), or P(top1%) is the metric that matters.

Recommendation: Confirm data provenance. Define the payout function. Evaluate whether opt_torvik's risk profile matches.

The Expansionist Expansionist

This is the beginning, not the conclusion. The Massey Ratings archive has 182 systems. Torvik is one. If one system produces a suggestive result, an ensemble of 3–4 uncorrelated systems should produce a robust one.

The catastrophic years are the real target. Single-system predictions are brittle. An ensemble dampens these failures. The path from p=0.027 to p<0.01 likely runs through system diversity, not through squeezing more out of Torvik alone.

Recommendation: After confirming Torvik data is clean, add KenPom and Sagarin. Build a simple average ensemble. Test whether catastrophic years improve.

The Outsider Outsider

Best of 6 at p=0.027 isn't significant after correction. That's the headline until it's resolved.

BestRank 159 means opt_torvik can't produce outlier wins. The seed baseline achieves BestRank 32.5 — top 3% of the pool in its best year. opt_torvik's best year is rank 159, top 16%. It's trading upside for consistency, and that's a bad risk profile if the pool rewards winning.

Recommendation: Audit the data first, but also consider whether "consistently above average" is what the payout structure actually rewards.

The Executor Executor

Check Torvik file provenance — one hour of work. Open the files, look at date stamps or column headers. If they say "Final" or show end-of-season dates, the backtest is contaminated and we stop. If they show pre-tournament dates, we have a clean foundation.

Information asymmetry is a feature, not a bug. The entire value proposition of using Torvik over seed-based brackets is that Torvik captures information the average ESPN pool participant ignores. That's how you beat the pool.

After confirming provenance, postmortem the 3 worst years. Identify which specific bracket picks drove the bad outcomes and determine whether those were systematic model failures or irreducible tournament variance.

Recommendation: (1) Check Torvik file provenance — one hour. (2) If clean, postmortem catastrophic years — two hours. (3) Then decide whether to ensemble or optimize further.
