Where the Council Agrees

Every advisor independently identified the opponent model's independence assumption as the critical flaw, and every advisor converged on collecting real bracket data (Option C) as the prerequisite to fixing it.

This convergence is trustworthy because the reasoning chains are independent: the Contrarian arrived via "backtest validity threat," the Statistician via "bias dominates variance," the Executor via "you can't fix what you can't measure," and the Outsider via "the #11 ranking is the tell." When four different analytical frames point to the same action, it's not groupthink — it's signal.

The council also unanimously dismissed Option B (bumping MC sims) as a standalone priority. The Statistician's framing: 5000 sims gives precise answers to the wrong question if the opponent model is misspecified.

Where the Council Clashes

The Existential Question vs. The Pragmatic Fix

The First Principles Thinker argues that with 14 data points and ~10% power, you can't know if any fix works — so the question is whether the system is worth improving at all. Four advisors and three reviewers pushed back.

Chairman's call: side with the majority. The power calculation is answering the wrong question. You don't need to prove the system beats random at p < 0.05 across 40 years. You need to answer: is the opponent model's independence assumption empirically wrong for your pool? That's testable with a single year of 31 brackets. You fix known modeling errors because they're wrong, not because you can prove the fix helps at 80% power.

BSS ≈ 0: Fatal Flaw or Irrelevant?

Three peer reviewers flagged that BSS ≈ 0 received zero scrutiny. The argument: if the base model has no skill, optimization maximizes noise.

Chairman's call: BSS ≈ 0 is a red herring. It means the model's probabilities ≈ seed-based probabilities — but seed-based probabilities are actually quite good (85-90% variance explained). The optimization layer exploits game-theoretic structure of winner-take-all pools, not prediction alpha. You don't need prediction edge to have portfolio construction edge.

Blind Spots the Council Caught

  • Your pool is not ESPN's population. ESPN aggregates represent ~20M casual brackets. Your 31-person pool likely skews more informed, more geographically clustered, or both. Collecting your actual pool's brackets answers whether ESPN distributions are even a valid proxy.
  • The #11 ranking is ambiguous evidence. Without examining what the top 10 brackets looked like vs. #11, you can't distinguish "opponent model overvalues contrarianism" from "MC noise scrambled close rankings."
  • 500-sim instability contaminates all cited backtest numbers. The 4.4% vs. 3.23% gap, rank correlations, and construction mode comparisons were all computed at 500 sims. These comparisons may not be reliable.

The Chairman's Take

The council got the diagnosis right but missed the sequencing nuance.

31 brackets from one year is enough to measure correlation structure but not enough to fit a correlation model. You'll compute pairwise pick correlations across 63 games for 31 people. You'll see picks cluster. But you won't parameterize a multivariate model that generalizes. What you'll learn: (a) how far your pool deviates from ESPN, (b) whether independence is qualitatively wrong, (c) what a reasonable "chalk clustering" parameter looks like. That's enough for a simple copula or correlation-block model — not a fully empirical simulator.

The opponent model fix and MC sim count are not independent. A correlated opponent model produces higher variance in simulated pool outcomes (opponents cluster, so your bracket either beats most or loses to most). Higher outcome variance means you need MORE sims for stable P(1st) estimates, not fewer. The Statistician's claim that "500 sims may suffice" after fixing the model is likely backwards. Fix the opponent model first, then empirically determine sim count needed — probably 2000-5000.
