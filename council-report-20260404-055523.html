The Question

Now that we have 19 years of real ESPN public picks data (2008–2026), can we proceed with testing EV pool optimization strategies? Or must we fix the team-name matching bug and other critical issues first? What is the overall status and readiness of this repo?

Chairman's Verdict

Where the Council Agrees

5/5 Unanimous

Do not run backtests until you know your match rate. The silent fallback to SEED_PICK_RATES is not a minor bug — it could be a total pipeline failure. The feature may literally not function.

5/5 Unanimous

This is a small fix, not a large one. Every advisor estimated hours, not days. One diagnostic script, one afternoon. No architectural rework needed.

5/5 Unanimous

The "momentum" argument is a trap. Running backtests on broken data produces numbers you'll anchor on and rationalize keeping. Motion is not momentum.

5/5 Unanimous

Serial execution on critical path. Skip orphaned files, file cleanup, blend weights, stacking contamination. Fix the pipe first. Everything else waits.

Where the Council Clashes

1 vs 4

Will clean data produce signal? The Expansionist says near-random results are "bullish" — garbage in, noise out. Four peer reviewers flagged this as wishful thinking. Clean data might still show no edge.

Debated

Skip the blend or validate it? Expansionist wants ESPN-only (100/0/0). First Principles wants empirical validation. Executor says ignore weights for now. Consensus: validate first, then simplify.

Blind Spots Caught in Peer Review

  • Shape of failure matters, not just rate. 20% uniform fallback vs 20% concentrated in early rounds are completely different failure modes. Diagnostic must log failures by year, round, AND seed.
  • The fallback might be accidentally reasonable. If SEED_PICK_RATES roughly tracks real ESPN picks for most matchups, existing results may be noisy but directional — not completely fictional.
  • Source data integrity varies across 19 years. ESPN naming conventions have shifted (UConn vs Connecticut). Perfect matcher against corrupted source data is still broken.
  • 64-to-68 expansion in 2011. Pre-2011 tournament structure is different. This may break backtest assumptions independent of name matching.

The Recommendation

Fix the pipe before you run water through it. (5-0, not a close call)

Spend one focused session (2–4 hours):

  1. Measure the damage. Diagnostic script: match rates per year, failures by round/seed, what fallback returns, source data anomalies.
  2. Build a lookup table. Literal dictionary, ~120 unique tournament names. Not fuzzy matching.
  3. Add per-year validation tests. Fill the gap in the 203-test suite. Test what's fragile, not what's interesting.
  4. Re-run stochastic backtest. Compare against previous near-random output. Signal separation = product. Still random = architectural problem.

The One Thing to Do First

Write the diagnostic script that prints match rates per year, with failures broken out by round and seed.

Not the fix — the measurement. If match rates are 95%, you can be surgical. If they're 30%, the ESPN feature has never worked. The number determines everything downstream.

Advisor Responses

The Contrarian Contrarian STRONGEST 5/5

The team-name matching bug is not a "known issue to fix later." It is the load-bearing wall of the entire project, and it is cracked.

When team names fail to match, the system silently falls back to SEED_PICK_RATES — the same generic seed-based baseline proven to have zero predictive edge. Could be 5% failing. Could be 40%. You have no telemetry. You are flying blind and calling it a strategy.

The 60/30/10 weighting is a distraction. Arbitrary weights on real data are recoverable. Arbitrary weights where the real data channel silently drops to zero? That is a pipeline bug wearing a lab coat.

Do not run a single backtest until you have match-rate diagnostics. Every "plausible-looking number" generated before that is a liability, not an asset. You will anchor on those numbers. You will rationalize keeping them. That is how bad decisions calcify.

One script. One afternoon. No excuses.

The First Principles Thinker First Principles

The question isn't "fix bugs or test strategies." It's: do you have an opponent model at all?

If your opponent model is wrong, you don't have a contrarian strategy — you have a random one. Random strategies in bracket pools have negative EV after accounting for chalk bias.

You don't know your match rate. The weights are made up. You can't validate without ground truth.

The real first principle: before testing any strategy, you need proof that your opponent model is more accurate than the trivial baseline (seed lookup). If it isn't, everything downstream is theater.

You're not choosing between fixing and testing. You can't test what doesn't exist yet.

The Expansionist Expansionist

The broken matching isn't a blocker — it's the single highest-leverage unlock. 19 years of real ESPN public pick data is an extraordinary dataset nobody else has.

Fix matching, but treat it as the feature. Once match rates hit 95%+, you can validate the blend empirically, discover year-over-year drift in public pick behavior, and build contrarian signals that compound historically.

The near-random stochastic backtest is actually bullish — garbage data produced noise, clean data should produce signal separation.

Spend 2-3 hours on matching, then run ESPN-only opponent models. Skip the blend entirely.

The Outsider Outsider

You have a system where the entire value proposition depends on modeling opponents. The pipe between ESPN data and your model is broken, silently falls back, and nobody knows how often.

You could be running 100% on fallback data. That's not a minor issue — that's "the feature doesn't exist yet" masquerading as "the feature has a small bug."

The "momentum" argument is a trap. Broken backtests aren't momentum — they're motion.

203 tests with zero name-matching coverage tells me the test suite tests what's interesting, not what's fragile.

Get the match rate numbers. Everything else is premature.
