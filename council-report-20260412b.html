LLM Council Report

March Madness Pool Optimizer — 2027 Improvement Strategy

Question Posed to the Council
Given the March Madness bracket pool optimizer's current state after implementing 3 critical improvements (P(1st) ranking, F4 seed diversity, opponent model validation), what are the most critical next steps to improve performance for the 2027 tournament?
Critical Actions
  1. Run the rank-correlation diagnostic. For each of the 14 backtest years, compute the Spearman correlation between predicted P(1st) rank and actual simulated pool placement for the top ~10 bracket variants. Gate If correlation is near zero or negative, stop optimizing bracket construction and focus entirely on the opponent model. If positive, proceed to action 2.
  2. Fix opponent correlation in the simulator. Replace independent-per-game opponent sampling with a correlated draw model — at minimum, sample complete opponent brackets from the ESPN pick distribution (not game-by-game), and ideally introduce a correlation parameter reflecting how clustered real pool entrants are around chalk. Gate Re-run the rank-correlation diagnostic from action 1; the correlation should improve or at least not degrade, and P(1st) estimates should shift (if they do not shift, the fix is not biting).
  3. Bump MC sims to 5000 and freeze. Do this as infrastructure before evaluating any future changes, so that P(1st) rankings are stable across runs. Gate Run the optimizer twice with identical inputs and verify the top-5 bracket ranking is identical.

Chairman's Analysis

Where the Council Agrees

Every advisor independently identified the opponent model as the critical bottleneck — not bracket construction, not ML improvements, not simulation fidelity. The specific failure mode is identical across all five: independent-per-game opponent sampling ignores the fact that real pool entrants are correlated (same ESPN defaults, same media diet, same casual-fan heuristics). This convergence is trustworthy because it follows from the architecture itself. The bracket optimizer can only produce edge relative to what it thinks the competition looks like. If the competition model is structurally wrong, the optimizer is maximizing against a fiction.

Four of five advisors also converged on the backtest being too small (N=14) to validate fine-grained parameter choices.

Where the Council Clashes

MC sims (500 vs 5000): The Executor calls this a one-line fix. The Statistician says the variance from 14 backtest years dwarfs MC sampling noise. Chairman sides with the Executor for a different reason: bumping to 5000 buys diagnostic clarity (ranking stability), not performance. It's a prerequisite for evaluating any other change.

F4 cap (2 vs 1.5): The Contrarian calls this small-sample overfitting. The Executor says drop to 1.5. Chairman sides with the Contrarian. The historical ~1.5 average is itself a noisy estimate from 14 tournaments. Leave cap at 2.

Pool-specific intelligence: Three advisors push for collecting real pick data from opponents. Chairman agrees in principle but flags feasibility — ESPN pools don't publish individual brackets until after lock. The user should assess data availability before committing engineering effort.

Blind Spots the Council Caught

  1. P(1st) may be the wrong objective — expected rank or P(top-3) might be more stable targets for development.
  2. Opponent correlation invalidates current rankings — fixing it changes which brackets are "contrarian."
  3. No calibration check exists — nobody verified whether simulated P(1st) correlates with actual historical pool placement.

The Chairman's Take

The 2026 result (bracket ranked #11 at 4.4% P(1st) winning the pool) is not just "variance" — it's a direct calibration test. The real question is whether the distribution of outcomes across 14 years matches predictions. A Spearman rank-correlation test (predicted P(1st) vs actual placement) can be run today. If correlation is near zero, no amount of opponent model improvement matters.

The user enters one pool per year. Over a 30-year career at 11.2% P(1st), expected wins = 3.4 vs 1.0 at random. The edge is real in expectation but will never feel statistically significant in one lifetime. This means optimizing for robustness over raw P(1st) maximization. Every new feature is a chance to introduce a bug that costs more than the feature gains.

Individual Advisor Responses

The Contrarian Challenge assumptions

The 3.5x baseline claim deserves scrutiny — 14 data points, CI spans 1x to 10x. Seed strategy averages 1236 pts requiring zero ML, nearly matching torvik. The real flaw: the opponent model is the load-bearing wall built on distribution averages, not your actual pool.

Your 31 real opponents have knowable tendencies. The F4 cap at 2 vs historical 1.5 is small-sample tuning that overfits. The most critical next step is collecting data on actual opponents.

The First Principles Thinker Reframe the problem

The question asks "what improvements maximize P(1st)?" but that's the wrong frame. The right question is: what is the actual source of the 3.5x edge, and is it durable?

The opponent model is the bottleneck, not bracket construction. You're modeling the average ESPN user, not your specific 31 participants. The 14-year backtest is unfalsifiable with ~5 degrees of freedom. Build a lightweight tracker of actual opponents' historical picks.

The Statistician Quantify uncertainty

The 14-year backtest is the central methodological problem. N=14 gives essentially no power to distinguish strategies differing by a few percentage points. The 11.2% P(1st) is a simulation output, not empirical.

Independent opponent sampling is likely the largest uncorrected bias. Priority: fix opponent correlation, increase MC sims, resist adding features.

The Outsider Question the game

You're treating a 14-year, 1-pool-per-year experiment as if it generates meaningful optimization signal. Every improvement validated against that backtest is overfitting to noise.

The most honest next step is establishing what "better" means — what P(1st) improvement would you expect from each change? Pool-specific intelligence about your actual 31 opponents is underexplored.

The Executor Ship it

Three highest-leverage actions: 1. Fix opponent correlation (biggest structural lie). 2. Bump MC sims from 500 to 5000 (one-line config change). 3. Drop F4 cap to 1.5 one-seeds.

Constraint is N=14 — don't build anything with more than 3–4 free parameters.
