Advisor Responses

The Contrarian Contrarian

Everyone's about to say "wire the optimizer into the backtest." They're right, but they're skipping the real question: does your optimizer ACTUALLY produce different brackets than random sampling?

Before wiring anything, do a single-year sanity check. Take 2023. Run PoolOptimizer.optimize() manually. Look at the brackets it produces. Are they materially different from sample_model_brackets() output?

The optimizer could be well-designed but produce near-identical output to random sampling if the model probabilities and public pick rates are too similar. If model_prob ≈ public_pct for most games, leverage is near-zero everywhere, and optimization degenerates to random selection.

Also: the backtest uses alpha=0.5 seed-noseed blend for the MODEL but 60/30/10 for opponents. You're comparing apples to oranges.

Validate the optimizer produces meaningfully different brackets on ONE year before wiring it into 17 years of backtesting.

The First Principles Thinker First Principles

A pool optimizer needs exactly one thing: a gap between what you believe and what the crowd believes.

Your system has three potential sources of edge: (1) Better game probability estimates — proven dead, BSS=0. (2) Better opponent modeling — exists but only used for simulation, not differentiation. (3) Better portfolio construction — exists in code but never called.

The architectural problem is simple: you built a gun, loaded it, aimed it, and never pulled the trigger. The PoolOptimizer IS the strategy. sample_model_brackets() is just drawing from the same distribution the public uses.

But here's the uncomfortable follow-up: even after wiring the optimizer, Source 1 being dead means leverage = (same_number - same_number) × points = 0. You may need to artificially inject contrarian bias rather than relying on model superiority that doesn't exist.

The Expansionist Expansionist

The optimizer bypass is the obvious fix, but the REAL opportunity is what you do after.

You're sitting on 19 years of ESPN "Who Picked Whom" data with clean team matching. The chalk bias — public picking 1-seeds to F4 at 42% when true rate is 21% — is MASSIVE. That's a 2:1 mispricing in the most consequential rounds.

Build a "contrarian signal layer" that identifies systematic public mispricings. Use it to TILT model probabilities away from consensus. Optimize bracket construction for P(rank=1) not EV — Clair & Letscher (2007) showed this requires conscious differentiation.

Stop trying to predict better. Start trying to be strategically different.

The Outsider Outsider

The backtest wasn't DESIGNED to test the optimizer — it tested whether stochastic sampling from different probability models produces different pool outcomes. Answer: no, it doesn't. All three models produce the same performance. That's a valid finding: your probability models don't differentiate enough to matter.

Will wiring the optimizer change this? Maybe. The optimizer strategically SELECTS picks anti-correlated with the crowd. But if underlying probabilities match public consensus, it has nothing to work with.

The question isn't "why is the backtest random?" It's "do we have ANY information that differs from what ESPN's crowd already knows?" If no, then no architecture fix will help. You'd need external signal — like the chalk bias correction.
